Copy you can lift

Three lengths.

One line

Tape Looper is an 8-track tape-style live looper for Mac that takes you from an idea to a finished loop in under a minute: Neural Amp Modeler on the live input, mix to disk, and every track already a file you own.

Short · ~60 words

Tape Looper is an 8-track live looper for macOS, laid out like a classic tape console. Tap a tempo, hit record, and stack parts in real stereo with sends, varispeed and a master bus. Guitar runs through Neural Amp Modeler on the live input, printed to tape, with free amp captures from TONE3000. Hit REC TO DISK and the mix is a 24-bit WAV of the whole take. There is no project format and no export step: every track lands in a dated folder as 24-bit audio, named with its bar count and tempo, ready to drag into any DAW. $39, one-time, with a 14-day trial.

Long · the background

Tape Looper was built by a musician who kept losing ideas to load screens. The premise is narrow on purpose: it should always be faster to capture a phrase in Tape Looper than to open a DAW, and it should never cost you anything to walk away afterwards.

So it opens ready to record. You tap a tempo, choose your bars, and play. The app gives you a count-in and locks each pass to the grid, sample-accurate against your audio hardware rather than a software counter. Eight tracks, linkable into stereo pairs for keyboards and drum machines. Guitar through Neural Amp Modeler on the INPUT strip, printed to the armed track. Amp captures are free at TONE3000. Per-track sends to shared delay and reverb returns, which you can monitor through while you play. Varispeed and reverse on any track. A master section with a bus compressor, a brick-wall limiter and a one-button fade-out, so a take can end properly instead of just stopping. Hit REC TO DISK and the mix is a 24-bit WAV of everything you heard.

And then the part that matters most: when you're done, there is nothing to export. The session is already a folder: one 24-bit AIFF per track, stereo pairs written as single stereo files, plus the mix-to-disk WAV of the whole performance. Every file is named with the session, the track, its length in bars and the tempo, so a loop dragged into a DAW months later still explains itself. Open that folder in ten years and everything still plays, with or without a licence.

Tape Looper is $39 as a one-time purchase with a full 14-day trial, and runs on macOS 14 or later.

If you're looking for an angle

Three honest ones.

THE COMPARISON

Software that competes with hardware

The obvious rivals are $200–600 hardware loop stations. Tape Looper gives you a full screen instead of a two-line display, and your takes come out as files instead of living on an SD card.

THE TECHNICAL ONE

No software clock, anywhere

Loop position is derived from the audio hardware's own sample time, never a counter the app increments. That single decision is why overdubs stay aligned through an hour of layering.

THE HUMAN ONE

Built by one musician, for himself first

Every feature had to answer one question: does this shorten the path from "I have an idea" to "it's captured"? Several genuinely cool ones were cut for failing it.

Worth knowing before you review

The unflattering parts.

You'd find these anyway. Better you hear them here.

No MIDI or footswitch control yet

For a hands-free looping performance this is a real limitation today. You drive it from the keyboard and mouse. MIDI foot control with MIDI-learn is the next feature on the roadmap.

You set the tempo before you record

Tape Looper is tempo-first: you tap or type a tempo and a bar count, then play to a click. It does not yet derive a tempo from a freely-played phrase. That mode is on the roadmap; the current design is what makes the timing as tight as it is.

Mac only, and no plugin version

It's built directly on Core Audio, which is the reason for the timing behaviour above. There's no Windows or iOS port planned, and it is not an AU or VST. It's a standalone app that sits alongside your DAW rather than inside it.

NAM sounds best at 48 kHz

Neural Amp captures are almost always 48 kHz. The app will resample around other device rates, but a 48 kHz interface is the path we recommend for guitar through NAM.

It's not a DAW, and doesn't want to be

No arrangement view, no clip launching, no plugin hosting, no editing beyond undo and erase. If a review's frame is "can it replace Ableton" the answer is no, and that's by design. The pitch is what you reach for before the DAW.
